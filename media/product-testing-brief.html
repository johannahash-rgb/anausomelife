Product team packet

Older-teen product-testing brief.

Not “Is this labeled for teens?” but: Can an older teen with profound/high-support autism actually access it without the product becoming another demand? We use that language for our own family experience, not as a universal label or hierarchy.

What we evaluate

  • Number of steps between interest and payoff
  • Language, reading and sequencing demands
  • Fine/gross motor demands
  • Frustration risk when the product behaves unexpectedly
  • Sensory payoff: movement, sound, pressure, visual feedback, texture or cause-and-effect
  • Age presentation
  • Durability, cleanability, portability and safety
  • Caregiver setup, charging, subscriptions and hidden workload

Participation can be very simple

For food and activity products, a meaningful role may be opening, pouring, stirring, sprinkling, placing or assembling while an adult handles knives, heat and complex sequencing. We do not define success as unsupported independence.

Best-fit review units

Sensory + adaptive

Low-complexity movement, tactile and cause-and-effect products.

Visual + tech

Smart displays, routine tools, headphones, safety/location wearables and household tech that removes steps.

Adaptive dressing

Clothing, footwear and closure systems that simplify supported dressing.

Food participation

Meal, baking and cooking systems with substantial prep already completed.

Responsible claims

We can discuss usability, taste, texture, routine acceptance, setup, labeling, third-party testing information and value. We do not suggest that a vitamin, supplement, gadget or sensory product treats autism, epilepsy, behavior or another medical condition without responsible evidence.

Useful beyond one diagnosis

The testing questions - number of steps, language load, sensory predictability, motor demand, setup burden and age presentation - can also help neurodivergent families with different profiles. We report what we observed rather than assuming the same result for everyone.
