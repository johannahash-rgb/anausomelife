Family life · Adult siblings

The adult-sibling field notes

Come keep a little room for the things that are yours as siblings.

4 minute read · Practical field notes
In this guide
  1. Start with the relationship you have.
  2. Choose one thing that belongs to both of you.
  3. Say what you are agreeing to.
  4. Talk about the future before it becomes an emergency.
  5. Give each other room to be adults.
  6. A small arrangement that travels well.

Start with the relationship you have.

A shared song, a ridiculous old joke, a snack picked up on the way over. Adult siblinghood can include practical support and still have ordinary pleasures at its center. It can also include distance, disagreement and separate lives.

These notes are for adult siblings, including siblings who have disabilities themselves. They do not presume closeness or a particular caring arrangement. If the relationship is complicated, choose a level of contact that is workable and safe for everyone.

Choose one thing that belongs to both of you.

The playlist exchange

Offer a couple of songs or ask for a recommendation in the person’s usual way. Listen together, trade a link privately or make a small playlist for the next visit. Silence, a repeat request and a change of song all have a place. Keep the volume and equipment comfortable for the person using them.

The short snack run

Agree the place, order, support and way home before leaving. One familiar stop is enough. Check any current eating arrangements, and do not turn ordering into an unexpected test of independence. A takeaway brought to the house can be the smaller version when travel is too much.

The shared episode, game or interest

Pick something both people actually enjoy. Decide whether this is a whole episode, a few minutes, watching someone else play or simply being in the same room. Keep a clear way to pause. Participation need not look equal every minute for the time to feel shared.

Say what you are agreeing to.

If you offer practical help, name the job and its limits. “I can bring dinner on Thursday” does not quietly become “I can cover the evening.” If a visit includes sole support, agree that separately and make sure you have the knowledge, ability and backup it requires.

  • Time: when you are available and when you need to leave.
  • Responsibility: the exact task you are taking on.
  • Preparation: what you need to learn or receive beforehand.
  • Changes: who is contacted if the plan cannot go ahead.

A useful sentence is: “I can handle the library visit once a month if the transport and handover are arranged. I cannot be the last-minute backup.” The wording can stay kind and direct.

Talk about the future before it becomes an emergency.

Begin with what your sibling wants for their own life. Use the communication supports they need to take part. Then ask family members which responsibilities they are willing and able to accept. The Sibling Leadership Network’s future-planning resources provide a starting point for these wider conversations.

For a first meeting, choose one question: who knows the current support arrangements, where useful records are kept, or which people your sibling wants involved. End with one next step and a named person who has agreed to it. Nobody needs to promise a lifetime of support in a single conversation.

Keep formal questions about finances, benefits, housing, decision-making authority and legal documents with qualified advisers and the relevant service providers. A family conversation can identify a gap; this guide cannot settle those arrangements.

Give each other room to be adults.

Ask before taking or sharing photographs, retelling private stories or adding someone to a family message thread. Speak directly to your sibling rather than conducting every conversation through a parent. Ask what help is welcome instead of assuming your preferred solution is theirs.

For younger siblings in the family, keep ordinary sibling time separate from adult care responsibilities. An older child’s availability is not a substitute for the support the adults have agreed to provide.

A small arrangement that travels well.

From farther away, offer a specific recurring connection if you want one: a short Sunday call, choosing the next film, a birthday card or a visit planned well ahead. Check whether the format works for your sibling and who, if anyone, has agreed to help make it happen.

Finish a visit with a clear goodbye and a realistic next plan. “I will text about next month” is better than promising every Saturday when your calendar cannot support it. Use the caregiver handover only when a real care responsibility is being transferred.
