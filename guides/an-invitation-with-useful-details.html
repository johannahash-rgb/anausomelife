A good question · Advice · 3 minute read

Dear An AUsome Life: what should the invitation say?

An editorial example about welcoming guests with clear timings, access details and room to ask privately for what they need.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide
  1. Give the day a shape
  2. Describe access without guessing who needs it
  3. Ask about food with a clear purpose
  4. Make partial attendance genuinely welcome
  5. Send a short update if something changes

This is an editorially constructed question, written to work through a common hosting situation. It is not a letter submitted by a reader.

The question: “We are having a small holiday lunch. I want guests to feel welcome, but I do not want the invitation to sound like a list of rules. What information actually helps?”

Start with the details that let someone decide whether and how to come: where, when, what will happen and how to ask a question. Warmth and useful information fit perfectly well in the same invitation. A guest should not have to solve a puzzle to learn when lunch will be served.

Give the day a shape

An arrival time and a meal time do different jobs. If you expect guests at noon and plan to eat at one, say both. Add an approximate finish if that helps people arrange travel or support. If the gathering is a drop-in, state the window and explain whether there will be a sit-down meal or food available at intervals.

Here is a simple constructed example: “We would love to see you for lunch on Sunday. Come from noon; we will eat around one and finish about three. Please let us know if you plan to join us.” Replace the details with the actual plan before using it.

Avoid “any time is fine” unless you mean it. A precise invitation can feel more relaxed because guests know what to expect.

Describe access without guessing who needs it

Share relevant facts you actually know. For example: “There are three steps at the front door” is clearer than “easy access.” If there is a different route, explain how to use it and confirm that it will be available. Describe parking, the distance from the car or a quieter place to sit when those are useful.

Do not advertise a home as accessible in every respect on the strength of one feature. A person may need details about the bathroom, seating or space for assistance that you have not considered. Give them a comfortable way to ask.

A useful line is: “Please message me privately if there is a practical detail that would help you decide or make the afternoon work.” You can offer examples without asking for a diagnosis or personal history.

Ask about food with a clear purpose

Tell guests what sort of meal is planned and ask about requirements you need to know. Be honest about what you can provide and what you need to discuss. If someone plans to bring familiar food, agree on storage, preparation and serving privately rather than making them negotiate at the table.

A host does not need to narrate one guest's preferences to everybody else. The invitation can make space for differences while keeping individual arrangements out of the group message.

Make partial attendance genuinely welcome

If a shorter visit works, say so directly: “You are welcome to join us just for coffee.” Then provide the likely time. Do not make the alternative sound like a consolation prize, and avoid following a guest's acceptance with pressure to stay longer.

For a gathering with gifts or photographs, explain any optional group activity in advance. A person can celebrate without agreeing to be photographed, to open gifts in front of a room or to participate in every tradition.

Send a short update if something changes

If the time, entrance or meal plan changes, tell confirmed guests clearly. A new detail buried in a busy group conversation may be missed. Repeat only the information that changed and anything people need to do.

Our family-life approach keeps choice and privacy in view. An invitation written that way gives guests enough information to make their own plan. It can still end with the simplest and nicest part: “We are looking forward to seeing you.”
