A one-page parent agenda you can actually use

A compact template for holding onto your priorities when the meeting begins to move quickly.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide
  1. Begin with the student, then the purpose
  2. Copy this working template
  3. Make each request easy to recognize
  4. Use the page during the conversation

A parent agenda can be a relief: the important things are written down, in an order you chose, before anyone starts speaking. Think of it as a practical companion to the school's meeting materials. It can help the conversation stay specific without becoming another long document to manage.

Keep the first version plain. Large, readable type and generous space for notes are more useful than decorative formatting. If one page becomes two because that is what you need to read comfortably, use two.

Begin with the student, then the purpose

Write the student's name on the private copy and the meeting date. Add one sentence about an interest, preference, or recent success that matters to the student. Avoid a catalogue of personal details. Share what helps the Team understand educational needs and participation.

Then write your purpose in one sentence: “I would like us to understand how communication support is working across the day and decide what information or changes are needed.” This is an illustrative example, not a recommended goal for every student.

Federal IDEA includes parents as IEP Team members and requires consideration of parent concerns in developing the IEP. Bringing an agenda is one practical way to organize that contribution. The Team still needs to work through the individual student's needs and the evidence. Team membership and IEP development.

Copy this working template

Use the prompts below in a document you keep privately. Replace the brackets before sharing it with the appropriate school team.

Our meeting

  • Student: [name]
  • Date and purpose: [meeting information]
  • Something important to know: [interest, preference, strength, or meaningful success]
  • What I hope we can accomplish: [one sentence]

My three priorities

  1. Topic: [specific part of the school day or educational need]. What I have noticed: [brief observation and date]. My question or request: [what you want discussed].
  2. Topic: [second priority]. Relevant information: [report, example, or missing information]. My question or request: [specific next step to consider].
  3. Topic: [third priority]. Why it matters: [effect on access, participation, learning, or dignity]. My question or request: [specific discussion].

Before we finish

  • What has been decided, and where will it be documented?
  • What remains unresolved or requires more information?
  • Who will do each next step, and what date has been agreed?
  • What documents should I expect, and who should I contact with questions?

Make each request easy to recognize

“Communication” is a subject. “Can we review how the student can make a choice during arrival, class activities, lunch, and dismissal?” gives the Team something concrete to answer.

Likewise, “More data” can become: “Please explain what was counted, during which activities, and with what level of assistance.” You are asking to understand the information, rather than assuming that a percentage has a single obvious meaning.

Separate your observation from your interpretation. “The report lists three opportunities” is an observation. “There may be too few opportunities to practice” is a question to explore. Both can belong on the page when the distinction is clear.

Use the page during the conversation

Send the agenda ahead if that is practical. At the meeting, ask to identify where the priorities fit into the proposed discussion. Keep your own copy visible and mark an item as discussed, answered, or still open. Those are different things.

If someone offers an answer that you do not understand, write a question mark rather than pretending it is settled. If time runs short, read the remaining requests aloud and ask how they will be handled. Avoid packing a major concern into an unexplained last-minute attachment.

Afterward, use your marked agenda to prepare a short follow-up note. Save the original and your notes separately so you can tell what you planned to ask from what you actually heard. A useful agenda earns its place by making the next conversation easier, too.

Educational information and practical organization ideas, not legal advice. Requirements and options depend on the student, location, and circumstances. Consult qualified local help for a specific dispute or deadline.
