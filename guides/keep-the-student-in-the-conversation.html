Keep the student in the conversation

Make room for preferences, communication, and dignity when the adults have a great deal to discuss.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide
  1. Describe the person before the paperwork
  2. Distinguish expression from interpretation
  3. Ask about communication across an ordinary day
  4. Plan participation that is useful to the student
  5. Bring the conversation back to daily life

A meeting about a student with extensive support needs can fill quickly with adult observations. There may be complicated schedules, personal-care questions, communication supports, and urgent practical concerns. The student's own experience can become harder to see in the middle of all that useful information.

A small preparation step helps: write down what the student enjoys, what you know about their preferences, how they communicate, and what remains uncertain. Keep those four categories separate. They give the discussion a human center without asking you to claim knowledge you do not have.

Describe the person before the paperwork

Begin with ordinary, age-respectful information: an activity they seek out, a familiar person they enjoy, a place they appear comfortable, or a way they participate in a shared routine. Interests do not have to sound academic to matter to the person.

Avoid reducing a teenager or adult student to a developmental-age comparison. Describe the specific assistance needed instead: support to make choices, help with transitions, accessible communication, or assistance with a daily activity. This keeps support needs visible while respecting the student's actual age and history.

When a personal-care issue affects school participation, identify the educational question and the people who need the details. Dignity includes thoughtful limits on who hears sensitive information.

Distinguish expression from interpretation

A useful note has three parts: what happened, the conditions around it, and what you think it may mean. For example: “When offered these two activities on three recent occasions, the student reached toward the music materials. We would like to understand whether this preference appears at school as well.”

This is an illustrative observation, not a universal method for determining choice. It leaves room for the student to respond differently in another context. It also avoids turning an adult's interpretation into an unquestionable statement of the student's wishes.

Include how much support was provided and who observed the response. If adults disagree about what a response means, name the uncertainty and ask what information or professional input would help. Avoid treating limited speech as evidence that the person has no preferences or nothing to contribute.

Ask about communication across an ordinary day

Choose a few moments to explore with the Team: arrival, shared activities, a break, lunch, or dismissal. Ask how the student can express a choice, seek help, refuse, or indicate that something needs to change in each setting.

Questions might include:

  • What ways of communicating are recognized across staff and activities?
  • What supports or equipment need to be available, and who checks that they are?
  • How do staff allow time for a response and record the assistance used?
  • What happens when familiar communication support is unavailable?
  • How are new staff or substitutes prepared to follow the student's plan?

These are educational planning questions to individualize with the Team. They are not a prescription for a particular communication system or treatment. Federal IDEA requires consideration of communication needs and whether assistive technology devices and services are needed. Read the special-factors provision.

Plan participation that is useful to the student

Discuss whether and how the student will participate in the meeting, taking account of applicable requirements, their preferences, communication access, and support needs. Federal IDEA includes the student on the IEP Team whenever appropriate. When a meeting will consider postsecondary goals and the transition services needed to reach them, the agency must invite the student. If the student does not attend, the agency must take other steps to consider their preferences and interests. Read the participation and transition provisions.

Participation might include joining a portion of a discussion or sharing an appropriately supported contribution prepared in advance. Do not assume those possibilities replace any requirement that applies to the particular meeting. Ask the Team how the student's perspective will be included and who will support access.

As adulthood approaches, clarify the applicable state rules about educational decision-making rights. Family involvement does not automatically establish decision-making authority after rights transfer. Read IDEA's age-of-majority provision.

Avoid requiring a polished performance to demonstrate that a preference matters. The purpose is to make the educational discussion more responsive to the person whose day it will shape.

Bring the conversation back to daily life

Before moving on from a proposed support, ask: “What will this look like during an ordinary school day?” Then ask how the Team will know whether it is helping the student access learning and participate.

Keep a short record of what is known, what was inferred, and what the Team agreed to explore. That modest habit protects both dignity and accuracy. It makes space for substantial support needs and an individual life in the same conversation.

Educational information and practical organization ideas, not legal advice. Requirements and options depend on the student, location, and circumstances. Consult qualified local help for a specific dispute or deadline.
