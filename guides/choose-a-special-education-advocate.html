Choosing an advocate: the questions behind the title

A thoughtful way to compare experience, judgment, fees, and fit before you hire help.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide
  1. First, understand what the title tells you
  2. Compare three areas of fit
  3. Use a simple comparison card
  4. Notice promises that deserve a second look

An advocate can help you organize a complicated situation and prepare for a clearer conversation with school. The right fit depends on what you need: understanding reports, identifying questions, attending meetings, or making sense of a disagreement. Start with that task before you start comparing impressive biographies.

For a family supporting a student with profound autism or extensive support needs, relevant experience should include more than familiarity with a diagnosis. Ask how the person approaches communication access, significant daily support needs, individual preferences, and age-respectful educational participation.

First, understand what the title tells you

The Federation for Children with Special Needs explains that advocates can help families understand special education and school records, and may attend Team meetings. Its Massachusetts guide also notes that there is no formal certification or licensing process for advocates and that most are not trained as lawyers. A course certificate should therefore be understood in context, rather than treated as proof of government licensure or legal authority. Read FCSN's advocate-selection guidance.

Ask what a credential means, who issued it, what training or assessment was involved, and whether any separate professional license is being claimed. Check a claimed license with the relevant licensing body. Requirements and permitted activities can differ by location; get local advice about legal representation.

Compare three areas of fit

Experience with the work you need

Ask: “What experience do you have helping families with needs similar to ours, and what would you want to learn before suggesting a plan?” A useful answer explains a process and acknowledges the limits of what can be known during an introductory call.

You can also ask which school systems or settings they know, how they stay current, and whether they have relationships that could create a conflict of interest. Experience with a district may be helpful, but a personal connection is no guarantee of an outcome.

How you will work together

Try these questions:

  • How do you include the student's preferences and communication?
  • How do you distinguish observations, educational recommendations, and legal questions?
  • May I review emails or documents before you send them on my behalf?
  • What happens when you and I disagree about an approach?
  • When would you recommend consulting an attorney or another specialist?

Ask for an anonymized description of their process, not another family's private records. References, when available and appropriately authorized, can help you learn whether the advocate communicates clearly and follows through.

What the work will cost

Request a written explanation of the rate, billing increments, minimum charges, and any retainer. Ask whether reviewing files, travel, preparation, phone calls, brief emails, cancellations, and post-meeting notes are billable. Clarify who does the work and whether different staff have different rates.

Then describe a bounded first task: “Please estimate the time and cost to review these documents and have one preparation call. Contact me before going beyond that scope.” An estimate may change when more information emerges; agree in advance on how changes will be approved.

Use a simple comparison card

For each person, record:

  • Relevant experience and any independently verified credentials
  • First task they propose and the reason for it
  • What they will deliver, and when
  • Estimated cost and possible additional charges
  • Communication expectations and your control over outgoing messages
  • Conflicts, confidentiality practices, and how the relationship can end
  • Questions still unanswered

You do not need a numerical score. “Explains clearly,” “uncertain about costs,” and “needs follow-up” may tell you more than a five-star rating.

Notice promises that deserve a second look

Be cautious with guaranteed placements, promises that a particular phrase will force a result, pressure to sign immediately, or advice to exaggerate. A careful advocate can be direct about a concern while remaining honest about uncertainty and the limits of their role.

Before paying, ask about available parent information and training. In Massachusetts, FCSN's special education resources are a useful starting point. Ask the organization about current support options, eligibility, and any charges rather than assuming all services or referrals are free.

The goal of an introductory conversation is a comprehensible next step. You should leave knowing what help is proposed, what it costs, and which decisions remain yours.

Educational information and practical organization ideas, not legal advice. Requirements and options depend on the student, location, and circumstances. Consult qualified local help for a specific dispute or deadline.
