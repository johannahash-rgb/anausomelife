We used to bring muffins. What do we do on video?

An example question about keeping the warmth of an in-person meeting while making room for clear requests.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide

  2. Keep the welcome small and genuine
  3. Give the first two minutes a little structure
  4. Make your own small meeting station
  5. Have a graceful interruption ready

An example question

“We used to bring muffins to the IEP meeting. It made the room feel a little more human before we started. Now everyone appears in a tiny square. How do we begin without feeling awkward?”

This is an editorial example, not a submitted letter. The feeling behind it is familiar: the small rituals around a meeting can help people settle in, and a video call sometimes removes all of them.

Keep the welcome small and genuine

You can preserve the spirit of that gesture with a short hello, an accurate thank-you, or a brief observation about the student. You do not need to replace muffins with mailed gifts, an elaborate presentation, or a performance of cheerfulness.

Try: “Good morning. Thank you for making the time. We appreciated receiving the report in advance. I have three questions I would like us to work through today.”

Only use the thank-you if it is true. If the past week has been difficult, a simple “Thank you for joining” is enough. Parents do not have to be charming to participate meaningfully, and access to appropriate education should never depend on refreshments or social ease.

Warmth and a firm request can occupy the same sentence: “I appreciate the work that went into this. I still need us to address how the plan will work during lunch.”

Give the first two minutes a little structure

Ask for names and roles, particularly when someone has joined by phone or is using a shared device. Confirm when the meeting is expected to end and whether anyone needs to leave early. Then explain what you hope to cover.

An example opening:

“Before we start, could everyone introduce themselves and their role? I would like to begin with a short update about what is working, then discuss communication access and transitions. Could we leave a few minutes at the end to confirm decisions and next steps?”

That final sentence makes a request about how to use the time. It does not promise that every question will be resolved in one sitting. If the agenda is too full, ask which items will be addressed now and how the remaining discussion will happen.

Make your own small meeting station

Set out the same things you would want at a physical table: your agenda, the current IEP, a pen, water, and a place to write unfamiliar terms. Close unrelated tabs before sharing your screen. Keep the meeting link and a contact number where you can find them if the connection drops.

You can ask for a dial-in option, a technology check, captions, interpretation, or another arrangement that would help you participate. Explain the barrier rather than assuming the school knows it. Under federal IDEA, parents and the public agency may agree to video or conference-call participation. Read the alternative-participation rule.

The school also has responsibilities to support parent participation, including scheduling at a mutually agreed time and place. A virtual invitation alone does not solve every participation problem. Read the parent-participation rule.

Have a graceful interruption ready

Video calls make it harder to notice when somebody is trying to speak. Choose a plain phrase you can use without apologizing repeatedly:

  • “I have a question before we move to the next section.”
  • “The audio cut out. Please repeat the proposed change.”
  • “Could you put that document on screen and identify the section?”
  • “I am hearing two different descriptions. Could we clarify which is correct?”

If you need a pause, ask for one. If you are upset, you can name the practical need: “I need five minutes, then I would like to continue with this question.”

At the close, thank people for a specific helpful action if you wish, then confirm what remains open. The most useful replacement for the muffin ritual may be very ordinary: a welcome, a readable agenda, and enough room for everyone to understand what happens next.

Educational information and practical organization ideas, not legal advice. Requirements and options depend on the student, location, and circumstances. Consult qualified local help for a specific dispute or deadline.
