Prepare for an IEP meeting in three short sittings

A calmer way to gather your thoughts, choose your priorities, and arrive with a manageable plan.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide
  1. Sitting one: describe the school day you want to understand
  2. Sitting two: choose three priorities and their evidence
  3. Sitting three: make the meeting easier to use
  4. Leave space for the unexpected

A school meeting can occupy far more than the hour on the calendar. There are reports to locate, observations to explain, and questions that tend to arrive just as you are trying to sleep. A useful preparation routine gives those thoughts somewhere to go.

Try three short sittings, separated if necessary by a day. The aim is a clear contribution to the conversation. A beautifully assembled binder is optional.

Sitting one: describe the school day you want to understand

Start with a fresh page and three headings: going well, difficult, and unclear. Write what you have actually observed, then mark what you learned from someone else. A home observation can be valuable even when the school sees a different pattern.

For a student with extensive support needs, ordinary moments often make the strongest starting points: arriving, communicating a refusal, waiting for lunch, joining a shared activity, using the bathroom privately, or leaving with the right equipment. Describe the support involved, not just whether an activity was completed.

An illustrative note might read: “At home, choices are easier when two objects are offered with time to respond. What choice-making opportunities are available during the school day?” This invites comparison without pretending a home observation answers every school question.

Include something the student enjoys and how they show interest. Use age-respectful language. A teenager who needs hands-on assistance is still a teenager, with a developing history, relationships, and preferences.

Sitting two: choose three priorities and their evidence

Circle the three matters you most want the meeting to address. Keep other concerns on a second page so you do not have to carry them in your head. Prioritizing is an organizing choice; it does not mean other needs cease to matter.

For each priority, collect one or two relevant items:

  • A dated observation or communication from school
  • The related IEP section or goal
  • A recent progress report or assessment finding
  • A short question about information that is missing

You do not need to prove a complete case before asking a question. “I cannot tell from this report how much help was needed” is a useful observation. Ask who can explain the information and what additional information would help the Team decide.

Under federal IDEA, an IEP Team must consider the student's strengths, parent concerns, evaluation results, and academic, developmental, and functional needs. That is a reason to bring your observations into the discussion, without assuming that every requested service will necessarily be adopted. Read the federal IEP-development rule.

Sitting three: make the meeting easier to use

Confirm the date, expected length, participants, and how you will join. Request the documents you need and any access arrangements, such as interpretation or readable electronic copies. Send your short agenda ahead when possible and keep a copy in front of you.

Massachusetts note: upon request, assessment summaries must be available to parents at least two days before the relevant Team discussion under 603 CMR 28.04(2)(c). This provision is specifically about assessment summaries; it is not a universal two-day deadline for all meeting papers. Ask early and identify the reports you need. Read the Massachusetts evaluation regulation.

Prepare two sentences you can return to:

“Today I would like us to understand [need] and discuss [request]. Before we finish, could we review the next steps and who is responsible for each?”

This is an example script to adapt privately. It is fine to read it aloud.

Leave space for the unexpected

Put a blank page beside your agenda. If new information arrives, write the document name, the question it raises, and whether you need time to review it. Ask for plain-language explanations. You can say, “I need a moment to understand that,” or, “Could we return to the question about support during transitions?”

When energy is very limited, use the smallest version: one priority, one supporting observation, one question. That is meaningful preparation. Your knowledge of the student's daily life belongs in the room whether you arrive with three pages or three sentences.

Educational information and practical organization ideas, not legal advice. Requirements and options depend on the student, location, and circumstances. Consult qualified local help for a specific dispute or deadline.
