The useful follow-up note after an IEP meeting

A short record of your understanding, open questions, and next steps, with an adaptable email template.

By An AUsome Life · Editorial guide
In this guide
  1. Separate four kinds of information
  2. An example email to adapt
  3. Keep the note distinct from official documents
  4. Check the documents against your notes
  5. Put one next step on your calendar

After a full meeting, details can blur quickly. A short follow-up note helps you preserve what you heard while the conversation is still fresh. It can also reveal a misunderstanding early, before people begin working from different assumptions.

Give yourself a little time to settle if the meeting was difficult. Then work from your agenda and notes, rather than trying to reconstruct every sentence. The most useful summary is often half a page.

Separate four kinds of information

First, list what you understood to be decided. Use careful language if the decision or documentation is not yet clear: “My understanding is that…”

Second, list actions with a named role or person and an agreed date, if one was actually agreed. If no date was set, ask for one. Do not turn your preferred timeline into a claim about a legal deadline.

Third, identify unanswered questions or points of disagreement. “We still need an explanation of…” is more informative than a broad statement that the meeting was disappointing.

Fourth, identify documents you expect to receive. A proposed change discussed aloud, a draft document, and a finalized plan may have different meanings. Ask where the decision will be recorded and what response, if any, is needed from you.

An example email to adapt

Subject: Follow-up to [student's] IEP meeting on [date]

Hello [appropriate school contact],

Thank you for meeting with us on [date]. I am writing to check my understanding of the discussion.

  • Decisions: My understanding is [brief, factual summary].
  • Next steps: [person or role] will [action] by [agreed date]. Please confirm, or let me know the anticipated date if we did not establish one.
  • Still open: We still need to address [question or disagreement]. Our request is [specific action or discussion].
  • Documents: Please let us know when to expect [document] and whom to contact if we have questions about it.

Please let me know if I have misunderstood anything. I would also appreciate confirmation of how we will address the open item.

Thank you, [Name]

This is a template, not a message from an actual family. Send a completed version only to the appropriate recipients through your normal private communication channel.

Keep the note distinct from official documents

Your summary is your record of the discussion. Silence in response should not be treated as agreement, an amendment, or a waiver of anyone's rights. Keep the school's official documents and read them separately.

Federal IDEA permits certain IEP amendments without a new meeting when the parent and agency agree to that process; a casual recap should not be assumed to complete it. Ask how any proposed change will be formally documented. Read the IEP-amendment provisions.

For proposals or refusals concerning identification, evaluation, educational placement, or the provision of a free appropriate public education, IDEA has prior-written-notice requirements. That notice must explain the action and its basis, among other things. A parent's follow-up email is not a substitute for the agency's required notice. Read the federal notice rule.

Check the documents against your notes

When the written material arrives, compare it with the specific items you discussed. Check names, dates, descriptions of needs, proposed changes, and any part you found confusing. If something differs, identify the document and section in your question.

Try: “In the proposed plan dated [date], section [section] describes [wording]. At the meeting, I understood [different understanding]. Please clarify what is being proposed.” This invites a precise response and preserves the distinction between memory and text.

Put one next step on your calendar

If a reply or document was promised by a date, make a reminder to check. If nothing was agreed, ask for a practical response date and follow the school's communication process. A self-chosen follow-up date is an organizing tool, not a statutory deadline.

For an important unresolved disagreement or a time-sensitive document, seek qualified local advice promptly. The goal of the note is clarity and continuity. It should make tomorrow's next step easier to see.

Educational information and practical organization ideas, not legal advice. Requirements and options depend on the student, location, and circumstances. Consult qualified local help for a specific dispute or deadline.
