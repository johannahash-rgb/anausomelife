We Don’t Fall Back Overnight

The clocks change in an hour. Our bodies, routines and disability supports do not.

November 1, 2026 · An AUsome Life

At 2 a.m., the clocks changed.

By breakfast, the rest of the country seemed to expect our family to have changed with them.

We had not.

In our house, the end of daylight saving time is not a neat one-night event. It is a transition—usually a slow one, spread across at least a week.

We follow the new clock for school, work, appointments and anything else that has to happen outside our front door. But inside our home, we cannot strictly move every meal, medication reminder, bathroom routine, calming activity and bedtime by a full hour overnight simply because the numbers on the clock changed.

That is not stubbornness. It is not bad parenting. It is not a refusal to teach flexibility.

It is disability accommodation meeting biology and ordinary family life.

The clock changes. The routine doesn’t.

Our son, James, is autistic and disabled, with significant support needs. Predictability helps him understand what is coming, communicate, regulate, eat, sleep and participate in family life.

Routine is not an ornamental extra in our home. It is part of the access structure.

Breakfast means more than food. It is a familiar beginning. Dinner, medicine, quiet time, the bathroom, favorite objects, the order of the evening and the path toward bed all tell the body and brain where we are in the day.

When the clock moves back, 6 p.m. may suddenly arrive when the body believes it is 7. Hunger does not consult the microwave display. Tiredness does not check the government calendar. Darkness outside can make the afternoon feel finished before the household is ready.

For some people, that is mildly annoying. For James—and for plenty of other people with disabilities, sleep differences, chronic illness or strong reliance on routine—it can be much more disruptive.

Autistic and disabled people are not a monolith. Some people barely notice the time change. Some enjoy it. Some are knocked sideways by it. This is our household’s experience, not a rule about everybody else.

Routine is an access support, not a preference

People sometimes hear “he needs routine” and translate it as “he should be pushed harder to tolerate change.”

But routine and flexibility are not opposites.

Routine can be the stable ground that makes a change possible. It can reduce the number of things a person has to decode at once. It can preserve enough energy for school, communication, relationships and the unexpected parts of the day that nobody can prevent.

Routine is not the enemy of flexibility. Sometimes it is the ramp that makes flexibility possible.

A gradual transition is still a transition. We are still moving toward the new time. We are simply not demanding that a disabled nervous system, a teenager’s sleep, two adults’ bodies and an entire household schedule make the jump in one night.

The body does not read the clock

There is a biological piece to this, too.

Our bodies use light, sleep, meals, movement and repetition as time cues. The wall clock can change in a second. Those signals cannot.

That is why the much-advertised “extra hour of sleep” has always seemed a little fictional to me. If your body wakes at what used to be 6:30, there is an excellent chance it will now wake at 5:30.

Then breakfast feels late. Dinner feels late. Bedtime feels late. And somehow the person who woke an hour earlier is expected to remain cheerful for an hour longer.

That is not a bonus hour. That is a scheduling problem wearing a party hat.

We change time slowly

Our goal is not to pretend the time change did not happen. Our goal is to make the new time understandable and tolerable.

We usually move the parts of the day we control by about 10 minutes at a time. We may shift breakfast, dinner, quiet time and bedtime a little each day. Sometimes we hold the same time for two days because the previous step was enough.

We preserve the sequence even while the clock time moves: dinner, bathroom, medicine if scheduled, calm activity, bedroom, sleep. Familiar order can do a great deal of work while the numbers change around it.

Our rule: protect regulation first. A peaceful bedtime matters more than winning a contest with the clock.

Medication is its own category. Some prescriptions depend on consistent intervals or specific clinician instructions. We do not independently move medication timing merely to make the household schedule look tidy; questions about changing it belong with the prescriber or pharmacist.

Outside the house still runs on the new clock

Of course, the school day, appointments and work do not wait for our household to finish transitioning.

Those obligations move immediately because they have to. We make the rest of the day lighter around them.

  • We prepare clothing, bags and breakfast choices ahead of time.
  • We use visual information instead of repeating verbal explanations.
  • We keep familiar foods and comfort items close.
  • We build in more quiet and fewer optional demands.
  • We expect early waking, hunger or fatigue instead of treating it as misbehavior.

Adjusting to a new time and being required to function on that time are two different things.

How we use them: put New time beside the actual clock, then show only the next routine card. If dinner or bedtime is not yet happening, pair that card with Wait. Keep Help and All done available rather than turning the cards into a compliance chart.

A very short time-change script

  1. The clock changed.
  2. My body may still feel the old time.
  3. At home, meals and bedtime can move a little at a time.
  4. I can use Wait, Help or All done.

Medicine timing follows the person’s actual medical plan. Do not move a prescription schedule just to match the household clock without guidance from the prescriber or pharmacist.

These are picture communication supports, not official PECS® materials. Use the person’s established AAC, photographs or familiar symbols whenever those work better.

Flexibility can look gradual

I think we sometimes mistake abruptness for strength.

If someone can tolerate a sudden change without showing distress, we call that flexible. If someone needs preparation, visual information, smaller steps or more recovery, we can be tempted to treat the accommodation as avoidance.

But a bridge is still a way across.

We are not teaching James that time never changes. We are teaching the change in a form his body and brain can use.

That is what accessibility often looks like: the destination remains, but the route is made possible.

We’ll get there. Just not by Monday.

By the end of the week, our meals and sleep may be close to the new schedule.

Or we may need a few more days.

The clocks will survive.

What matters to me is that our home remains a place where disability is not treated as an inconvenience to the schedule, and where the schedule serves the people who live here—not the other way around.

So yes, we observe the time change.

We just observe it slowly.

Ten minutes at a time. With visual supports, familiar routines, medication handled according to the actual medical plan, and as much grace as we can manage before breakfast.

Does your household change the clocks all at once, or do you need a longer transition? Share what helps—especially the small accommodations other families may not think of.
