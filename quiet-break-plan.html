Build a quiet-break plan before you need it

A break works better when nobody has to invent it in the middle of a crowded place. Decide where, how and what comes next while everyone is still calm.

In this guideChoose the placeChoose the cuePlan the next stepRedefine success

Find two possible pause points.

Ask the venue where a lower-stimulation break can happen, then identify a second ordinary option such as the car, a bench outside or a quieter corner. A designated sensory room may be excellent, but it is not the only kind of pause that can help.

Make the cue simple.

Use the person’s familiar communication. The cue can be a word, symbol, gesture, photo or device button that means “break,” “outside,” “car,” “finished,” or another phrase they already use. Keep the communication method available rather than introducing a new system for the outing.

Decide what happens after the break.

A break does not have to mean the day is over, and returning does not have to be the goal. Before you go, choose a few valid options: return to the main activity, switch to the shorter version, get food, or go home.

Keep the success measure human.

Staying longer is not automatically better. Watching first, doing one part, taking several breaks or choosing to leave can all be successful participation. Build the plan around the person, not the admission price.
