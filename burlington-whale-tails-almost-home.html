South Burlington, Vermont · From the family album

Almost home.

Burlington’s whale tails, then and now. A decade of lake days, friendship and belonging—and a return from Massachusetts with James. With parking, walking access and nearby-highway planning notes.

An AUsome Life · Published October 22, 2026 · A memory from an earlier family visit

The storyParking & walking accessHighway & elopement planningMore photographs & sources

For years, two whale tails sticking out of a Vermont field meant I was almost home.

I lived in Burlington for a decade, and Lake Champlain was my everything. Being near the water brought me peace and helped ease my anxiety. I could look out over the lake and feel a little more room inside myself. That kind of comfort stays with you.

My friends were just as much a part of what made it home. When I think about Burlington, the people and the place come together: the friendships, the lake, the feeling of belonging to a community. It is a community like no other I’ve known, surrounded by a beauty I still miss.

That is what the whale tails were welcoming me back to. There they were beside I-89, familiar and wonderfully improbable. I couldn’t tell you how many times I passed them over those years. I can tell you what seeing them meant: nearly there. Back to the lake, my friends, my life.

They are technically in South Burlington, but to me they belong to that feeling of arriving in Burlington. Two enormous whale tails in the grass. In Vermont. Of course.

They are so perfectly Burlington: art out in the open, a little oddness, a love of the natural world, and something worth looking at on an otherwise ordinary drive. I love that they manage to be both a surprise and an old friend.

Now home is in Massachusetts. Getting back to Vermont takes a little more doing, and being able to make that trek with James means a great deal to me. There is something wonderful about bringing your child to a place that already holds years of your life. You arrive with all those memories, and then you get to add him.

That is what I see in this photograph. James on the green path, smiling, with tall grasses on either side and the whale tails up behind him. All that blue sky. A place I have carried around in my mind, with my son right there in the middle of it.

For so long, the tails were something I passed on my way home. Getting out and walking toward them with James made them a destination in their own right. I love having a photograph of that. When I think of the whale tails now, I get to think of this day, too.

The sculpture has a proper name: Reverence. Jim Sardonis created it in 1989, carving the tails from African black granite. They spent their first ten years in Randolph before moving to South Burlington in 1999. At roughly 12 to 13 feet high, they have a presence that a passing glimpse from the highway doesn’t quite convey.1, 2

And there really is a whale story beneath the Vermont landscape. The Champlain Valley was once occupied by the Champlain Sea. In 1849, railroad workers in nearby Charlotte uncovered the bones of a beluga whale. The University of Vermont tells the story of that discovery and the ancient sea that explains it.3 Whales in a Vermont field turn out to be a little less improbable than they first appear.

I appreciate that history. I appreciate the sculpture. But my attachment to those tails is also very simple: I saw them over the years, and they came to mean home.

Massachusetts is home now, too. Coming back with James lets those two parts of my life meet. I get to bring him into a place that gave me so much: friendship, beauty, and somewhere to find a little peace. I can be a visitor here and still feel the old recognition. There they are. I know this place.

And now I have this picture of James to add to all the years before it.

The practical details

Visiting the whale tails

Our photograph records an earlier visit. These planning notes were checked on October 22, 2026, using the artist’s directions, Technology Park and Trail Finder. Conditions and parking arrangements can change.

  • Where to go & parkReverence is at Technology Park in South Burlington. Approach via Community Drive off Kimball Avenue; Trail Finder places the trailhead on the right as the road curves. Use designated trail parking and follow posted signs. Reach the walking route through Technology Park.
  • Cost & walking distanceTrail Finder lists no fee and a 1.7-mile loop. That is the full listed loop, not a measured parking-to-sculpture distance. A Vermont Moms account describes a shorter walk directly to the tails. Follow the current marked route and turn back when it suits your family.
  • Ground & mobility accessThe listed surfaces are grass/meadow and packed earth/dirt. Our photo shows the uphill grassy approach. Trail Finder rates the route “easy,” but wheelchair and stroller suitability depends on the device, slope and ground conditions. A paved accessible route, accessible parking bays and visitor toilets were not confirmed in the sources checked.
  • Comfort & planningOur photo shows an exposed stretch with little shade. Bring water and weather protection, and allow for softer or slippery ground after rain or snow. With the interstate nearby, traffic sound is a reasonable planning consideration. Ask Technology Park about the current route and any access details your family needs.

A separate note for the visit

The highway, the distance & elopement planning

Yes—when you drive past the whale tails on I-89, you are on the interstate. That wonderfully unexpected highway glimpse was my sign that I was almost home. Technology Park places them between exits 12 and 13. To visit on foot, leave the highway and use the park’s trail approach. Do not stop on the interstate shoulder or try to walk in from I-89.

How close is the highway? Roughly 100 feet (30 metres) from the sculptures to the nearest paved edge of northbound I-89. This is our straight-line estimate from 2024 Vermont aerial imagery, checked for this story—not a surveyed distance, a walking route or a safety buffer. It is also entirely separate from the distance you walk from parking.

For a family planning around elopement or wandering, that proximity deserves attention. The CDC describes wandering as leaving a safe area or responsible caregiver, and identifies traffic as a danger. It can happen even with attentive supervision. This is a planning consideration, not a report of an incident on our visit.

Boundary fencing appears in reference photographs, but we have not verified its present condition or a continuous barrier around the walking area. Treat this as an outdoor trail beside a highway, rather than an enclosed play space. Plan the close support your child needs before leaving the car, agree who is staying with them during photographs, keep familiar communication supports available, and make room for “break” or “all done.” The photograph can wait.

If the setting does not suit your family that day, the passing view from a safely driven car can be the whole experience. There is no need to turn an old sign of home into an outing that asks too much.
