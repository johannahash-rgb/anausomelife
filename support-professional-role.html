Family life · Paid caregivers & direct support professionals

The support-professional field notes

Come help make an ordinary day work well for the person living it.

3 minute read · Practical field notes
In this guide
  1. Begin with the person, then the plan.
  2. Make the first few minutes useful.
  3. Build around a chosen piece of ordinary life.
  4. Make room for a real response.
  5. Warmth and clear professional boundaries belong together.
  6. Leave a handover someone can act on.

Begin with the person, then the plan.

A shift may begin with a list, but it belongs to someone’s day. Greet the person directly, learn what they are expecting and check the current information you need. Ask how they prefer to be addressed and what kind of support is helpful.

These notes are for direct support professionals, personal assistants and paid caregivers. Job titles, training, duties and employer requirements vary. Use the person’s current support arrangements and your authorized scope of work. An activity idea here does not authorise a care procedure.

Make the first few minutes useful.

  1. Read what changed. Check the current record and ask about anything that affects this shift.
  2. Confirm the day. Review the agreed plans with the person in an accessible way, including choices that are genuinely available.
  3. Find the essentials. Know where needed communication tools, supplies and current instructions are kept.
  4. Check the handoff. Be clear when responsibility transfers and who is available if you need guidance.

If a required support or instruction is missing, raise it before beginning the affected activity. Use the organization’s procedures and appropriate contacts rather than improvising a new plan for medication, eating support, transfers or other tasks that need specific instruction and training.

Build around a chosen piece of ordinary life.

The useful errand

Agree one destination and what the person wants to do there. Check the transport, access, spending arrangements and return plan. Support the person’s part of the errand without taking over every decision. If the original plan becomes unworkable, offer the alternatives that are actually possible.

The familiar meal preparation

Choose a task within the person’s current food and safety arrangements: selecting plates, gathering an agreed ingredient or setting the table. Provide the assistance they use. Do not introduce an unapproved food, preparation method or care technique because an activity guide suggests cooking together.

The interest that continues between shifts

Ask about books, music, a collection, a team, a favorite route or another ongoing interest. Find out where the last visit left off. The person may want you to join in, help with one part or stay nearby while they do their own thing. Respect the answer.

Make room for a real response.

Use the person’s established communication methods and allow the response time they need. Ask for clarification when you are unsure. Keep your observation separate from your interpretation: “They moved the cup away twice” gives the next person something concrete to understand.

When an option is unavailable, explain that in a usable way and offer the realistic next choices. Do not offer a decision that has already been made as if the person can change it. Refer to the communication field guide for everyday partner ideas, alongside any individual guidance.

Warmth and clear professional boundaries belong together.

The NADSP Code of Ethics addresses the person’s choices, privacy and professional responsibility. In everyday practice, clarify expectations for messaging, photographs, gifts, money, personal errands and contact outside working hours through the applicable workplace policies and the person’s arrangements.

Use authorized record systems. Keep private discussions out of public spaces, and do not post about a shift on personal social media. Ask the appropriate supervisor or contact when duties or permissions are unclear. Familiarity does not remove the need for consent, confidentiality or accurate records.

Leave a handover someone can act on.

  • What the person chose and what took place.
  • Any relevant change, described factually.
  • What needs to happen next, by whom and when.
  • Which required records, supplies or belongings need attention.
  • Any issue escalated through the proper route.

Complete required records and reporting within your organization’s procedures. The short handover template is a planning aid; it does not replace required documentation, incident reporting or an individual support plan.

Before leaving, confirm who is taking over any support that needs uninterrupted coverage. A friendly goodbye and a clear next step are both part of a well-finished visit.
