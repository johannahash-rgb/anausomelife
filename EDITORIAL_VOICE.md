# An AUsome Life — editorial voice & texture

## The voice
An AUsome Life should sound like a real person with taste, a sense of place and a lot of practical experience — not a lifestyle-brand generator.

**Warm, specific, observant, lightly wry.**
Classic without cosplay. Autism-aware without therapy-copy. Honest without tragedy framing. Pretty without pretending logistics disappear.

A useful test: could JoHannah plausibly say this out loud to a friend in the car? If not, rewrite it.

## What belongs in the voice
- Concrete observations over abstractions.
- Ordinary pleasures alongside practical constraints.
- Strong opinions stated calmly.
- New England as a lived setting, not a costume.
- A little dry humor about weather, parking, laundry, hotels, overbuilt systems, marketing copy and household logistics.
- Respectful acknowledgement of high support needs without turning the person into a lesson.
- Age-respectful language for teens and adults.
- Recommendations that feel earned through use.

## Humor rule
Punch at **objects, systems, logistics, expectations and ourselves as adults** — never at disability, communication differences, support needs or the person receiving support.

Good:
- “A lovely lobby is a bonus. A working pool and the right beds are rather more important.”
- “If a household system needs its own training manual, it may have become a second job.”
- “The trousers chosen three mornings in a row have conducted their own focus group.”
- “A family outing does not become morally superior because it lasted six hours.”

Avoid:
- jokes about meltdowns, communication, dependence, behavior, sensory needs or care tasks;
- “special needs humor”;
- inspirational punchlines.

## New England without caricature
Use actual details: wet leaves, an old farm, a harbor, the pool in February, the bakery stop, a muddy parking lot, good wool, a ferry schedule, the coat by the door.

Avoid generic “coastal grandmother,” lobster-everything, fake Yankee dialect, or turning the region into a theme park.

## Autism and disability
- Support needs can be significant and stated plainly.
- Speak to the person, not around them.
- Do not confuse support needs with personality, age, taste or intelligence.
- Do not turn ordinary family pleasure into inspiration.
- Do not imply that access is a favor.
- Use medical/therapy language only when the subject genuinely requires it.

## Copy patterns to prefer
**Specific:** “Is the pool warm? Is there a place to sit? Can we leave without crossing the entire building again?”

**Lived-in:** “The thing that gets chosen on an ordinary Tuesday tells us more than the thing that photographs best.”

**Dry:** “A robot vacuum that requires a pre-vacuum is a little cheeky.”

**Earned:** “We would buy this again” means more than “we love it.”

## Copy patterns to cut
- “thoughtful ways to…”
- “empowering families…”
- “beautiful and functional” repeated without specifics
- “navigate the journey”
- “creating meaningful moments”
- “designed to support…”
- long strings of caveats before the actual point
- generic “New England charm” language

## Page rhythm
No long page should become a wall of prose.

Aim for a visual or editorial change of scene every 2–3 screens:
- a photograph or illustration;
- a field-note aside;
- a short pull quote;
- a “what actually matters” card;
- a mini checklist;
- a map, route or real-world detail;
- a small family notebook moment.

The visual should clarify or reward attention, not merely decorate.

## Editorial north star
**Useful enough to save. Pretty enough to want to read. Real enough to trust.**
