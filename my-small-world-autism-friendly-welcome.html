My Small World · Bath, England

A welcome before you walk through the door.

A little toy-shop sign, and a very useful idea for every high street.

An AUsome Life · October 8, 2026

A photograph of a sign outside My Small World, a toy shop in Bath, England, caught my eye. It announces that the shop is autism friendly. Then it gets to the part I especially like: what that means when you actually arrive.

The sign offers to lower the music, soften the lighting and give people space. It reassures visitors about excited play, noise and moments of overwhelm. It invites families to say what would help.

That is a lovely thing to encounter before you have even opened the door.

The relief is in the details.

When an ordinary errand takes planning, a specific welcome gives you something to work with. You can picture asking for the music to come down. You know that needing a little room is something the staff have already considered. One less conversation has to begin with an explanation.

On its own website, My Small World explains that its team includes autistic people and parents of autistic children. The shop describes a commitment to helping families feel comfortable, grounded in that lived experience.

What stays with me is the invitation to arrive as you are. There is room in that idea for a wonderful browse, a very brief visit, a pause, or deciding to try another day.

Bring that welcome to our own Main Streets.

A bookshop, café, farm stand or hotel can make its welcome useful in the same way: tell people what help is actually available, and make it easy to ask. A few places to begin:

  • Be specific about sound and light. Say whether music can be lowered, which areas are quieter, and what lighting can be adjusted.
  • Offer time and space. Explain where someone can pause and whether they can step outside and return. Ask what helps before stepping in.
  • Welcome different ways of communicating. Give people time to point, type, use picture cards or speak through an AAC device. Address the person directly and respect their answer.
  • Keep the welcome age respectful. Autistic teenagers and adults belong in the picture, too. Offer help without assuming someone’s interests, abilities or preferred language.

Those are ideas for other businesses to consider, beyond the adjustments described on this particular sign. The best version is a promise the staff understand and can keep.

A small sign can start a conversation.

For our family, the practical details belong right alongside the lovely ones. The invitation to look around matters. So does knowing that asking for a change will be met with understanding.

I would love to see more of that on our own Main Streets: a clear, ordinary welcome, with enough detail to make walking through the door feel a little easier.

About this note

This is a reflection on a sign spotted online and the shop’s published approach, rather than a report from a family visit. The sign has circulated for years; we are sharing the idea, not announcing a new policy.

Read My Small World’s story and autism-friendly approach or contact the shop before a visit. Official information checked October 8, 2026. Ask directly about the adjustments and access details that matter to you.
