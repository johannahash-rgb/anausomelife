A family field note · Rancatore’s, Lexington

A scoop,
a phone,
and a choice.

At Ranc’s, we photographed the flavor case and tried a picture-based way to show James the options. Here’s what we made—and what another family or scoop shop could take from it.

By An AUsome LifeOctober 7, 2026Lexington, MA
The family storyPhoto → choice boardWhat needed checkingFor your next scoopFor scoop shopsFree picture cards

The place: Rancatore’s, 1752 Massachusetts Avenue, Lexington. Check the shop’s current information or read our visit and parking notes.

The resource: our own photo-to-choice experiment, plus free editable picture cards below. The illustrated board is an example from our outing—not today’s menu, a shop-provided service or evidence of a formal accessibility program.

Originally published October 7, 2026. Practical family, retailer and sharing notes expanded October 19, 2026; this update does not describe a new visit.

The choice belongs to the customer

A case full of ice cream offers plenty to choose from. The question we brought to Ranc’s was how to put those choices in a form James could consider.

We were heading to Rancatore’s in downtown Lexington—Ranc’s, as we call it—and I had my phone with me. James, my 14-year-old son, is autistic and non-speaking. He communicates in ways that include pointing and using his iPad, so clear pictures are part of our everyday life.

We had already made a little visual board to choose where to go. Ranc’s, Cookie Monstah, and Wilson Farm each had their own picture. Once we reached the ice cream shop, there was another decision waiting for us: the flavors.

Rather than try to recite the entire case out loud, I photographed the actual displays and shared them with AI. The idea was simple: bring the choices off the labels and onto a screen we could look at together.

Our finished page was not a perfect menu or a finished communication system. It was a first attempt that needed checking. The photographs below show the source and the original result, including the parts we would simplify another time.

We ended up with a photo just outside, an ice cream cone in hand. That ordinary afternoon—not the technology—is the point of the story.

Before the ice cream counter

First we chose where to go.

We started with a visual destination board: Cookie Monstah in Burlington, Ranc’s in Lexington and Wilson Farm in Lexington. Each place had a familiar storefront picture rather than a generic picture of a cookie or an ice cream.

Then, at Ranc’s, we made the combined cone-and-flavor board shown above. Its original layout keeps three cone choices at the top and the labeled flavors below. Another version could show these separately rather than presenting everything at once.

The technology wasn’t making the choice for James. It was helping us show him the choices.

A few things I learned

Fast doesn’t mean finished.

The AI was quick, but it wasn't automatically right. It can invent a flavor, misread a handwritten label, or make a beautifully crowded page. Our large flavor board was a first pass; a shorter display of the choices James wanted to consider might be easier to navigate.

The real photos gave us something concrete to check. The board was never meant to replace James’s established augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) supports, or his ability to say no, ask for something different, or take a break.

Sometimes the best visual is simply the photograph you already took. A picture cannot establish ingredients or allergen safety: ask staff about ingredients and cross-contact rather than inferring either from a label or illustration.

An optional tool, not the whole method

A prompt to try.

“Use these photos to make a draft picture choice board. Copy only labels you can read clearly, and flag anything uncertain instead of guessing. Do not add flavors, cone types or ingredient claims. Use clear labels and leave space to separate the flavor choice from the container choice.”

A careful prompt is not a guarantee. Check every option against the current display and staff guidance before using the result. Remove unavailable items and change pictures or labels that do not suit the person.

No AI needed: use a checked photo of a few actual options, or arrange your own photographs. Avoid capturing other customers or uploading private information.

For your next scoop

Bring the choices closer.
Keep the outing ordinary.

This is what we would take from our Ranc’s experiment into another visit. Adapt the idea to the person—not the person to the board.

Start with today’s options.

Ask what is available, then use clear photographs or familiar pictures. Our old flavor board is a record of an experiment, not a shopping list for your visit. Check ingredients separately with staff.

Make one question manageable.

Try flavor and cup-or-cone as separate choices. Start with a manageable display while keeping a way to ask for other options. A shorter board should not silently remove the person’s right to choose something else.

Leave time—and room for “no.”

Keep the person’s usual communication available. Accept pointing, a device, speech or another familiar response without turning ordering into a test. Changing a choice, taking a break and declining are choices, too.

Let a scoop be enough.

Decide where eating will work and how to finish the visit. You do not need a second activity or a perfect photograph to make an ice cream stop worthwhile.

For independent ice cream shops & retailers

The flavor case is a menu.
A photograph can be another way in.

The useful question for a shop is not whether it needs an AI system. It is whether a customer can find out what is available, take the time they need and communicate a choice in their own way.

Keep choices current.

A simple, current picture menu or clear photo of available flavors can be worth trying. Use readable labels, mark sold-out items and check information with staff. Pictures should not substitute for ingredient and allergy information.

Welcome the customer’s tools.

Address the customer, not only the person accompanying them. Make room for a phone, communication device or picture board, and ask what assistance would be useful rather than assuming.

Offer another way to look.

When feasible, let someone consider the choices away from the immediate ordering position without losing their opportunity to order. Do not assume that a customer who needs more time needs fewer choices.

Describe what you actually offer.

Share concrete details about ordering, seating, entrances and any verified quiet-hour arrangements. A good individual visit is not the same thing as a formal sensory-friendly program or a universal accessibility guarantee.

A distinction worth keeping: these are suggestions drawn from our family’s perspective. We are not claiming Rancatore’s has adopted them, provided our board or endorsed this guide. Shops can share the story as a conversation starter without making those claims.
