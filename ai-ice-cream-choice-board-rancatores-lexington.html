How a few photographs at Ranc’s became an on-the-spot visual choice board for James.

By An AUsome LifeOctober 7, 2026Lexington, MA
The little storyPhoto → choice boardThe cone questionTry it yourselfOur photos

An ordinary afternoon, with a new idea

Some families make visual supports at their kitchen table. On this afternoon, I wondered if I could make one at the ice cream counter.

We were heading to Rancatore’s in downtown Lexington—Ranc’s, as we call it—and I had my phone with me. James, my 14-year-old son, is autistic and non-speaking. He communicates in ways that include pointing and using his iPad, so clear pictures are part of our everyday life.

We had already made a little visual board to choose where to go. Ranc’s, Cookie Monstah, and Wilson Farm each had their own picture. Once we reached the ice cream shop, there was another decision waiting for us: the flavors.

Rather than try to recite the entire case out loud, I took photographs of the actual displays and shared them with AI. Could it turn what was in front of us into pictures James could consider on the spot?

We ended up with a photo just outside, an ice cream cone in hand. It is a reminder of what the exercise was for: not a perfect piece of design, but an ordinary afternoon with room for James’s choices.

One board, two decisions

The actual layout matters.

At Ranc’s, the sheet we made had both questions on the same page: Choose my cone at the top, with regular, waffle and cake cones; and Choose my flavor below, with labeled pictures inspired by the real ice cream case.

That is the full board shown above, in the same order and layout as the original—not two different storyboards. Before the shop, we also made a separate destination choice board with familiar storefront pictures for Cookie Monstah, Ranc’s and Wilson Farm.

The technology wasn’t making the choice for James. It was helping us show him the choices.

Why we kept the order

Where first. Then what.

The destination board helped make the outing options visible. Once we got to the ice cream shop, the cone-and-flavor sheet put the actual ordering decisions together, in a form James could look at and point to.

Nothing here replaces his established communication tools or the need to check whether the pictured items are available.

A few things I learned

Fast doesn’t mean finished.

The AI was quick, but it wasn't automatically right. It can invent a flavor, misread a handwritten label, or make a beautifully crowded page. Our large flavor board was a useful first pass, but for actual use, a shorter selection might be far easier to navigate.

The real photos were important because they gave us something concrete to check. And while the board was new, it was never meant to replace James’s established AAC supports or his ability to say no, ask for something different, or take a break.

Sometimes the best visual is simply the photograph you already took.

Save this idea for your next outing

A prompt to try.

“Use these photos to create a simple visual choice board. Show only the options that are actually available. Use large, recognizable pictures, clear labels, and separate the flavor choice from the cone choice. Do not invent items.”

Before using it, check the names and pictures, remove anything unavailable, and make sure the layout suits the person who will use it.

Good for: ice cream shops, bakeries, cafés, farm stands, playgrounds—anywhere the options change from day to day.
