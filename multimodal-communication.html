The Communication Field Guide · Updated October 2026

Communication is an ecosystem, not a device.

A word. A look toward the door. A familiar gesture. The actual cereal box. A photograph on a phone. A symbol. A typed sentence. A speech-generating device. A switch. A sound. More than one of these, in the same hour. Multimodal communication means keeping enough reliable routes open that a person can say what they mean in the place, body, energy level, and moment they are actually in.

The polished answer is not always the useful one. Sometimes the right communication tool is sophisticated technology. Sometimes it is the photograph you took in the parking lot thirty seconds ago. Prestige is not the point. Being understood is.

01 / The whole toolbox

There is no prize for using only one route.

Augmentative and alternative communication is broader than a particular app or device. ASHA describes AAC as multimodal: people may combine speech or vocalizations, gestures, signs, facial expression, body movement, objects, photographs, visual supports, writing, text, symbols, communication boards or books, speech-generating technology, and different access methods. The mix can change with context, audience, purpose, motor access, fatigue, and sensory conditions.

A good system therefore asks two questions at once: What does this person have to say? and what is the easiest reliable route for saying it right now?

Aa

Speech + vocalization

Words, approximations, sounds, laughter, intonation, and other intentional vocal signals.

✋

Gesture + sign

Pointing, reaching, conventional gestures, manual signs, and familiar movements.

◉

Gaze + body

Eye gaze, facial expression, orientation, movement, and established body signals that can be confirmed.

●

Real objects

The actual cup, shoe, snack, ticket, towel, or item in front of you can make a choice concrete immediately.

▧

Photos + visual scenes

Camera-roll pictures, venue photos, screenshots, visual schedules, maps, and pictures of real choices.

✎

Drawing + writing

Sketching, handwriting, note apps, typing, texting, saved phrases, and text-to-speech.

▦

Symbols + low-tech AAC

Symbol displays, communication books, printed backups, and small situation-specific boards alongside robust language.

▣

Speech-generating technology

Dedicated devices, tablets, and phones with robust symbol- or text-based communication systems.

↳

Switch + scanning access

Direct or indirect access that lets a communicator select from available choices using an established signal.

The field rule

Do not make speech the tollbooth.

Speech is one route. It should not be the price of admission to choice, attention, participation, humor, refusal, privacy, or an ordinary conversation.

More than requesting

A full communication life includes asking, refusing, commenting, complaining, joking, greeting, telling news, correcting someone, asking questions, changing the subject, seeking help, saying “not that,” and ending the conversation. A system that can only produce “I want” is carrying far too little of the person’s life.

Behavior can tell you that something changed. It cannot safely tell you the whole sentence.

Pacing, movement, vocalization, withdrawal, reaching, or a facial change may tell a familiar person that something is different. Treat that as information worth responding to, not as permission to assign a precise meaning. “Something seems different—can we figure it out?” plus an accessible way to answer protects agency better than deciding what the person meant.

02 / The useful art of improvising

Use what is already around you.

Communication does not stop because the perfect material is in another room. Real objects, signs, menus, packaging, maps, tickets, photos, screenshots, timers, calendars, a notes app, a dry-erase board, and the environment itself can all make a message easier to understand or express. These are additions and backups—not reasons to take away a person’s established AAC.

At a café

Use the actual menu.

Point to categories. Show the picture of the soup. Compare two real items. Let a person point, type, use their device, gesture, or say “none of these.” Keep food choice, size, drink, and “finished” as separate decisions.

At the grocery store

The shelf is already a visual display.

Hold up two packages. Point to the cart. Use a saved photo of the familiar brand. Photograph a new item so it can be talked about later. The real object often carries more information than an abstract placeholder.

On an outing

Photograph the next step.

Take a picture of the entrance, the ticket booth, the pool door, the car, or the bench where a break can happen. A phone camera can turn an unfamiliar sequence into a concrete one in under a minute.

At home

Let the house do some of the talking.

Use the actual cereal box, bath towel, shoes, calendar, timer, laundry basket, or remote. Pair words and AAC with objects already attached to the routine.

In a medical office

Make the invisible visible.

A body diagram, mirror, photo, typed note, or showing the relevant body area can reduce language load. Prepare words such as stop, break, hurts, not sure, explain again, and where.

Online or in a group

Use the side doors.

Chat, advance questions, a shared document, typed responses, and a follow-up message can make participation possible when real-time turn-taking is too fast.

A communication tool already in the room still counts. The aim is not to create a Pinterest-perfect support system. The aim is to make meaning easier to send and receive without taking control away from the communicator.

The camera roll is a surprisingly good communication drawer.

Photos can set a topic, show a person or place, represent a choice, clarify which item someone means, prepare for a visit, or help recall what happened. Use current, meaningful pictures where possible. A photograph of the actual pool is often more useful than a generic blue square labeled “swim.”

Visual supports can explain what language alone leaves fuzzy.

A short sequence can show where we are going, what happens first, what happens next, where a break can happen, and how we get home. Keep the sequence flexible enough for real life. If something changes, show the change rather than pretending the original plan is still true.

03 / The person across the table matters too

The communication partner is part of the system.

Research on AAC partner instruction repeatedly points to partner behavior as consequential. The device can be charged, the vocabulary can be excellent, and communication can still collapse if the people nearby rush, quiz, answer for the communicator, or only notice conventional speech.

Speak to the person.Ask the communicator the question. A supporter can help with access or context without becoming the person being addressed.
Wait longer than feels natural.Looking, motor planning, navigating vocabulary, composing, or switching routes takes time. Silence is not an emergency.
Model, do not quiz.Use the communication system while you talk when appropriate. Demonstrate language without turning every interaction into a test.
Make “no” work.Offer choices that can genuinely be refused. Include stop, break, finished, not sure, and something else.
Respond to credible attempts.A word, look, gesture, device selection, typed message, or established signal deserves a response even when the answer to the request is no.
Repair honestly.“I think I missed that. Can we try another way?” is better than pretending to understand.
Do not move communication out of reach.Ask before touching or relocating a personal communication device. Communication access should not be treated as a reward.
Check the environment.Noise, glare, seating, positioning, battery, volume, Wi-Fi, visual clutter, and fatigue can all make a familiar route much harder.
Field note no. 02

Partner skill can lower the effort of being understood.

Good support does not make the message for the person. It makes room, time, access, and another route when the first one is difficult.

04 / Redundancy is civilized

Choose a primary route and a backup before you need the backup.

Electronics lose power. Sunlight makes screens hard to see. Pools are wet. Restaurants are loud. Motor access can change with fatigue. A familiar staff member is absent. Planning two workable routes ahead of time keeps an ordinary problem from becoming a communication emergency.

Before leaving the house

  • The person’s primary communication tools are charged, mounted, packed, or otherwise available.
  • A low-tech or lower-effort backup covers the urgent messages that matter in this setting.
  • At least one adult knows the person’s reliable yes/no or selection signals.
  • Situation-specific words have been added without taking away access to the rest of the person’s language.
  • There is a plan for water, glare, noise, a dead battery, a broken mount, or a device that suddenly refuses to cooperate.
  • The person can refuse, change course, and end the activity—not only request it.

05 / Ordinary places, different demands

Build the route around the setting.

Pool

Keep the device dry. Keep communication wet-proof.

A waterproof or laminated backup can cover stop, more, out, bathroom, cold, help, finished, and something else. Establish how yes/no will work in the water. Keep the full system nearby in a safe dry place.

Restaurant

A menu is both language and clutter.

Use the actual menu, photos, pointing, text, or a short display. Separate the decisions: entrée, side, cup or glass, more, finished. Keep “none of these” available.

School or program

Consistency is not sameness.

Keep core language and access available across rooms and people. A brief communication profile, charging plan, backup, and access notes make the system less dependent on one familiar adult.

Medical visit

The historian should not replace the patient.

Ask the person first when possible. A supporter can clarify history and access while still leaving room for the patient’s own yes/no, stop, pain, fear, not sure, and questions.

High stress

Lower effort, not expectations of personhood.

Stress may make a usual communication route harder. Offer a familiar lower-effort route, reduce competing sensory demands, wait, and keep urgent messages easy to reach.

New people

Put the interaction rules on one small card.

“Please speak directly to me. I communicate using [routes]. Give me about [time] to answer. If that route is hard, try [backup]. Please do not [ ].”

06 / AI can do the clerical work

Let AI format the support. Do not let it invent the person.

AI can be genuinely useful for turning known facts into a clean card, simplifying a venue’s information, organizing a visual sequence, formatting current choices, creating a plain-language version, or drafting a handoff. The safe dividing line is authorship: the tool may help organize and present information; it should not claim to know what the person thinks, wants, understands, or feels.

Good use

Turn real facts into a visual plan.

Give the AI the actual location, sequence, photos or descriptions, break option, and way home. Ask for a short plan using only those facts.

Good use

Make current choices easier to scan.

Paste today’s actual menu or activity choices. Ask the AI to group them clearly without adding unavailable options.

Good use

Produce two reading levels.

Keep the meaning the same while asking for a compact card and a fuller staff note. The details stay consistent; the presentation changes.

Good use

Translate a known message.

Translation can help bridge languages. Preserve the original alongside the translation when precision matters and verify important wording.

Do not

Ask AI to read someone’s mind.

Do not turn a photo, behavior, movement, or supporter’s hunch into a claimed statement of what the person wants or feels.

Do not

Upload private history because the prompt has an empty box.

Use the minimum information needed for the communication task. A restaurant host does not need a medical chart to wait fifteen seconds for an answer.

07 / Agency first

Communication belongs to the person.

Support can make access easier without taking over the message. The aim is not for a partner to become a better guesser. The aim is for the communicator to have reliable ways to initiate, choose, refuse, clarify, disagree, repair, and end an interaction.

Final selections stay with the communicator.Suggestions, prediction, modeling, or visual organization can reduce effort. The person still chooses the message.
Interpretation is labeled as interpretation.If a supporter thinks a behavior may mean discomfort, say “I wonder if…” and seek confirmation rather than presenting the guess as the person’s statement.
Unexpected answers are allowed.A communication system should make it possible to disagree with the supporter, pick the option nobody predicted, or introduce a new topic.
Repair is shared work.When meaning is unclear, both people can slow down, change route, add context, and try again.
Field note no. 03

Presume a right to communicate, not a message you have already decided.

Respect means access, time, useful language, and the freedom to surprise the person who thought they knew the answer.
